1. Scope and sources

The database covers every pardon and commutation granted from January 20, 1989 to the most recent grant on record. Each record includes the president and term, the type of clemency, the date granted, the recipient, the sentencing district and date where reported, and the offense as described in the official record.

Records come only from official sources: the Department of Justice Office of the Pardon Attorney, whitehouse.gov and the Federal Register. The researchers check the Office of the Pardon Attorney's lists for new grants and record the date of each check.

2. Defining white-collar crime

There is no single legal definition of white-collar crime. The rubric draws on four sources and asks whether an offense falls under any of four categories: white-collar crime, corporate crime, fraud or unethical business practices. These usually involve nonviolent conduct, often in a business setting, aimed at financial gain through deception.

Table 1. Definitions used in the rubric SourceApproach Edwin SutherlandCrime committed by a person of respectability and high social status in the course of their occupation. U.S. Department of JusticeNonviolent crime for financial gain committed by deception, using occupational or specialized business and government knowledge. U.S. Sentencing CommissionLists of included offenses (for example tax, environmental, antitrust, embezzlement, mail and wire fraud, counterfeiting) and excluded offenses (for example theft of mail by a postal employee). White-collar practitionersDefined by exclusion: offenses that do not involve force, narcotics, organized crime, policy-driven crimes such as immigration and civil rights, or vice.

3. Classification process

The rubric, developed by Professor Todd Haugh, is applied to the offense text of each grant. It considers the nature of the conduct, the people or entities involved, any legal or regulatory violation, financial motive, and potential victims. Each application produces four fields:

Table 2. Rubric output fields FieldValues White collarYes or No CategoriesWhite-collar crime; Corporate crime; Fraud; Unethical business practices. "None" when No. ExplanationA justification of no more than three sentences ConfidenceHigh, Medium or Low. "Not rated" only for unnamed placeholder records.

Classifications for newly added grants are produced with the help of an AI model applying the same rubric. These records are marked "Needs review" until a researcher confirms or corrects them.

7. Known limitations

  • Jan. 6 pardons. About 1,500 recipients were not named individually and are represented by {%placeholders%} placeholder records, so second-term Trump totals that include them are approximate.
  • Biden group warrants. The {%biden_warrants%} commutations of {%biden_warrant_dates%} are counts only.
  • 2020 election pardons. The 77 pardons by proclamation of Nov. 7, 2025 are counted as individual grants.
  • Vague offenses. 17 records describe the offense too vaguely to classify with certainty, and no official source resolves them. They carry a best-reading classification at Low confidence.
  • Offense text only. Classifications rely on the offense as officially described, not on court files or the facts of each case.

8. Review and verification

  • Second opinions. {%second_opinions%} records received a second, independent determination. Where the two disagreed, the rubric was re-applied and the record was flagged for review.
  • Open items. {%needs_review%} records are marked "Needs review" and are labelled as such on their record page until a researcher signs off.
  • Totals check. Term totals are reconciled against president totals each time the data is updated.
  • Exclusions. {%excluded%} records duplicate a grant that is already counted and are excluded from all figures (listed below).

9. Using the data

  1. Filter to include_in_analysis = Yes before counting. Excluded rows are duplicates of grants already counted.
  2. To reproduce the headline white-collar share, also filter to mass_action_group = None.
  3. Treat second-term Trump totals that include the Jan. 6 placeholder rows as approximate.
  4. Check data_status and confidence before relying on a single record; records marked Needs review may change.

Appendix A. Full rubric text

Key passages of the rubric are reproduced below, condensed for the web. The complete text is available on request from ICG@iu.edu.

Show the full rubric

As an expert in criminal law, corporate law, corporate governance, and business ethics, your task is to analyze the following text and determine if it pertains to any of these specific areas of misconduct: (1) white-collar crime; (2) corporate crime; (3) fraud; (4) unethical business practices.

These areas of misconduct usually involve nonviolent crimes, often committed in a business setting, designed to achieve financial gain through deception or fraud. They are often described by the blanket term “white collar crime.” Some more specific examples include public corruption, health care fraud, mortgage fraud, securities fraud, and money laundering, but do not limit the definition to only these offenses because the definitions of the targeted misconduct can vary.

[The rubric then sets out the Sutherland, Department of Justice, U.S. Sentencing Commission and practitioner definitions summarized in Table 1.]

Based on all this, please carefully examine the content and context of the provided text. In your analysis, consider the following aspects: the nature of the actions or behaviors described; the individuals or entities involved (e.g., executives, employees, corporations); any mention of legal or regulatory violations; financial implications or motivations; potential victims or affected parties.

Your response should be structured as follows: (1) start with a simple yes or no; (2) main category/categories, if applicable; (3) explanation: a brief justification of no more than three sentences; (4) confidence level: High, Medium or Low. If the text is ambiguous or lacks sufficient information to make a definitive judgment, please indicate this in your response. Remember to maintain objectivity and base your assessment solely on the information provided in the text.

Appendix B. Glossary

Pardon
Presidential forgiveness of a federal conviction.
Commutation
A reduction of a federal sentence. The conviction remains.
Individual grant
A grant that is not part of a mass action. The basis for the headline white-collar share.
Mass action
Grants issued to a large group at once by proclamation or group warrant. See section 5.
Term year
Year 1 to 4, counted from inauguration. Grants on a president's last day count in year 4.
Confidence
How certain the white-collar determination is, given the offense text: High, Medium or Low.
Placeholder record
A stand-in row for an unnamed Jan. 6 pardon recipient, so that totals are complete.
